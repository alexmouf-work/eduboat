# Syllabus

**Status: agreed in scope, 23 August 2026.** The specification the site is built to deliver. Ledger
item **E002**.

**Where the content comes from.** The principal supplied the term list on 23 August 2026: **106
entries across 11 groups**. Every one of them is in scope and none has been added to or removed,
because charter §6.3 makes the syllabus's scope the principal's decision and not an agent's. Where
this document proposes something further, it is in §14 and marked as a proposal awaiting a decision.

**What has been changed from the supplied list, and why.** Two things, both sequencing rather than
scope:

- **The parties were pulled out of "Counterparties and documents" and made module 1.** Every other
  module says "the owner pays" or "the charterer directs", so the roles have to be settled before any
  of it means anything. The paper and compliance half of that group stayed together as module 11.
- **Dimensions and loading now comes before vessel classes.** A Panamax is defined by a beam and a
  draft, so the measurements have to exist before the ladder of classes can be more than a list of
  names to memorise.

**What this document is not.** It is not the lessons. It is the list of what must be taught, in what
order, with the dependency between modules made explicit, so that a lesson can be written against a
stated obligation rather than to taste. The lessons live in the content tree once E005 creates it.

---

## 1. How this is taught

Every term is taught to the standard in charter §4.9, which is worth restating because it is what
separates this from a glossary:

- **The mechanism, not the gloss** (MT3). Who pays, to whom, under which contract, and what changes
  if it is wrong. A one-line definition is an index entry.
- **A source and a confidence mark on every claim** (MT1, MT2). Marked `settled`, `varies` or
  `unverified`, and the mark is shown on the page. Where a term varies, the lesson names what it
  varies with.
- **Worked examples arrive at a number** (MT4), with a unit on every quantity.
- **The market's word, exactly** (N4). The learner is taught the word the desk uses, not a plain
  English paraphrase of it.

The voice is [`../charter/teaching-copy-style.md`](../charter/teaching-copy-style.md).

**Two tracks, and they can be learned in parallel.** Modules 1 to 6 and 11 are the commercial track:
who the parties are, how a deal is struck, and how the money works. Modules 7 to 10 are the physical
track: what a ship is, what constrains it, and what happens in port. They meet at module 6, where the
time charter equivalent turns the physical facts into a comparable daily number, and again at module
12.

**Module 6 is the keystone.** Time charter equivalent is the number every desk argues about, and it
cannot be computed without the voyage economics of module 4, the hire mechanics of module 5, and the
ballast leg from module 10. If one module gets the fullest treatment, it is this one.

## 3. Module 1 — The parties

**Rests on nothing. Everything rests on it.**

Four roles, and the whole point of the module is that they are frequently four different companies
and are routinely confused for one.

- **Owner** — registered owner of the vessel
- **Operator** — commercial manager running the trading
- **Technical manager** — crewing, maintenance, class compliance
- **Charterer** — the party hiring the ship, or the cargo interest

**What the lesson must establish**, beyond the four definitions: that "owner" in a fixture usually
means whoever is contracting as owner, which may be a disponent owner rather than the registered one
(taught in module 3), and that the broker sits between the parties and is paid by commission rather
than by either side's salary (module 2).

---

## 4. Module 2 — The desk: how a deal happens

**Rests on module 1.** The narrative spine of the whole syllabus: this is the sequence a working day
follows, and every later module is detail hung off one of these steps.

- **Fixture** — a concluded charter deal
- **Fixing** — the act of doing one
- **Order** — a charterer's cargo requirement circulated to the market
- **Position list** — which ships are open, where, and when
- **Open** — ship free and available at a given port and date
- **Ballasting from X** — steaming empty toward the next load port
- **Firm offer** — binding for a stated period
- **On subs** — agreed subject to remaining conditions (subjects)
- **Lifting subs** — conditions cleared, deal is binding
- **Failing subs** — deal collapses at that stage
- **Recap** — recapitulation of agreed main terms
- **Main terms** — the commercial core, agreed before the full charterparty
- **CP / charterparty** — the contract itself
- **Laycan** — layday and cancelling window; the ship must present inside it or the charterer may cancel
- **Commission / address commission** — the broker's cut, around 1.25%, and the charterer's rebate, around 2.5% to 3.75%

**What the lesson must establish:** the ordering. Order circulated, tonnage matched from the position
list, indications, firm offer with a validity, counters, agreement on main terms, on subs, subs lifted
or failed, recap, then the charterparty drawn up. The learner should be able to say at any point in
that sequence whether a binding contract exists.

**Confidence note.** The commission figures are conventions and are marked `varies`. They are what the
market ordinarily runs at, not what a fixture must contain.

## 5. Module 3 — Charter contract types

**Rests on modules 1 and 2.** The single most important distinction on the syllabus, because it
determines who pays for what, and every economic term later means a different thing under each.

- **Voyage charter** — owner carries cargo A to B for freight; owner pays bunkers, port costs and canal dues
- **Time charter (TC)** — charterer hires the ship per day; owner crews, maintains and insures, charterer pays bunkers and port costs
- **Trip time charter (TCT)** — time charter terms for the duration of one voyage
- **Bareboat / demise charter** — charterer takes the bare hull and does everything; effectively a finance lease
- **COA, contract of affreightment** — commitment to carry a stated tonnage over a period, with ships nominated as you go
- **Spot** — single voyage, prompt
- **Period** — fixed duration, six months, one year, five years
- **Disponent owner** — someone who charters a ship in and charters it out again; not the registered owner

**What the lesson must establish:** a who-pays-what table across voyage, time and bareboat, covering
bunkers, port costs, canal dues, crew, maintenance, insurance and commercial risk. That table is the
thing to memorise, and everything in modules 4 and 5 is an elaboration of one of its cells.

## 6. Module 4 — Voyage charter economics

**Rests on module 3.** The owner is paid freight and bears the time risk, so time in port is money,
and laytime is how that risk is divided.

- **Freight** — payment for the voyage, USD per tonne or a lumpsum
- **Deadfreight** — charterer pays for booked space not used
- **Laytime** — the time allowance for loading and discharging
- **NOR, notice of readiness** — the master declares the ship ready; it starts the clock
- **Turn time** — grace period after NOR before laytime commences, often 6 hours
- **SOF, statement of facts** — the hour-by-hour record of what happened in port
- **Demurrage** — daily rate the charterer pays for exceeding laytime; liquidated damages
- **Despatch** — owner pays the charterer for finishing early; conventionally half demurrage
- **"Once on demurrage, always on demurrage"** — laytime exceptions stop applying once demurrage begins
- **Worldscale (WS)** — tanker freight quoted as a percentage of a published notional flat rate per route
- **Flat rate** — the WS 100 reference figure for a given route

**Worked example required** (MT4): a laytime statement built from a statement of facts, arriving at
hours used, hours allowed, and a demurrage or despatch figure in USD.

**Second worked example required:** a Worldscale conversion, from a WS percentage and a route flat
rate to USD per tonne, and from there to total freight.

**Confidence note.** Turn time at 6 hours and despatch at half demurrage are conventions set by the
charterparty and are marked `varies`. Some fixtures exclude despatch entirely.

## 7. Module 5 — Time charter mechanics

**Rests on module 3.** The charterer is paying by the day, so the argument moves from time in port to
whether the ship performs as promised.

- **Hire** — daily rate paid by the charterer, usually 15 days in advance
- **Off-hire** — hire stops when the ship cannot perform: breakdown, drydock
- **Speed and consumption warranty** — the owner guarantees, for example, 14 kts on 30 mt/day; underperformance is a claim
- **Trading limits** — excluded areas: war zones, ice, sanctioned states
- **Delivery / redelivery** — the start and end points of the charter
- **BOD / BOR** — bunkers on delivery and on redelivery, bought and sold at agreed prices
- **Ballast bonus** — a lump sum compensating the owner for positioning to the delivery point
- **Employment clause** — the charterer directs the ship's commercial use within limits

**Worked example required:** a performance claim. Warranted speed and consumption against actual,
over a stated passage, arriving at a figure in USD, showing both the lost time and the excess bunkers.

**What the lesson must establish:** that hire and freight are different payments under different
contracts and are never interchangeable words. This is charter N4 applied to the two terms most often
confused.

## 8. Module 6 — Money and measures

**Rests on modules 4 and 5. The keystone of the syllabus.**

- **TCE, time charter equivalent** — voyage freight minus voyage costs, divided by total voyage days including ballast; expressed in USD per day. The universal comparator
- **Ton-mile** — one tonne carried one mile; the true unit of shipping demand
- **DWT, deadweight** — maximum cargo, fuel, stores, ballast and crew the ship can carry
- **Displacement** — total weight of the ship, equal to the weight of water displaced
- **Lightship** — the ship's own empty weight
- **TPC** — tonnes per centimetre immersion; how much cargo sinks her one centimetre
- **OPEX** — daily running cost: crew, stores, insurance, maintenance
- **Voyage costs** — bunkers, port dues, canal transit fees
- **Baltic indices** — BDI composite, BCI and 5TC for Capesize, BPI and P5TC for Panamax, BSI for Supramax; the USD per day benchmarks
- **FFA, forward freight agreement** — a cash-settled freight derivative against a Baltic index

**Worked example required, and it is the most important one on the syllabus:** a full time charter
equivalent for a dry bulk voyage. Cargo tonnage and freight rate in, bunkers, port costs and canal
dues out, laden days and ballast days counted, arriving at USD per day, then compared against the
relevant Baltic index level on a stated date.

**Second worked example required:** deadweight, displacement and lightship reconciled on one vessel,
with TPC used to show what one centimetre of draft is worth in cargo tonnes.

**What the lesson must establish:** why the time charter equivalent exists at all. A voyage rate in
USD per tonne and a time charter rate in USD per day cannot be compared, and every fixture decision
is a comparison. The comparator is the whole reason a desk computes it before answering an order.

**Braemar note.** The forward freight agreement is where the syllabus meets Braemar's securities and
futures arm, taught properly in module 12.

## 9. Module 7 — Dimensions and loading

**Rests on nothing. The start of the physical track.**

- **LOA** — length overall
- **Beam** — width at the widest point; decides which lock or berth she fits
- **Draft** — depth below the waterline; decides how much can be loaded
- **Air draft** — height above the waterline; the bridge-clearance constraint
- **Freeboard** — waterline to main deck
- **Load line / Plimsoll mark** — painted marks showing the maximum legal draft by zone and season
- **TF / F / T / S / W / WNA** — tropical fresh, fresh, tropical, summer, winter, winter North Atlantic
- **TFW draft** — tropical fresh water draft; relevant in Gatun Lake
- **Down to her marks** — loaded to the legal maximum
- **Trim** — the difference between forward and aft draft
- **List** — an athwartships lean
- **Squat** — the ship sits lower underway in shallow confined water
- **Under-keel clearance (UKC)** — the margin between keel and seabed
- **Cube out** — running out of space before weight
- **Weigh out** — running out of deadweight before space, with a dense cargo
- **Stowage factor** — volume occupied per tonne of a given cargo

**Worked example required:** a stowage factor calculation showing the same ship cubing out on one
cargo and weighing out on another, with the cargo tonnage arrived at in both cases.

**Second worked example required:** the load line marks applied to a real routing, showing how the
zone and season change the legal draft and therefore the cargo, and how fresh water allowance changes
it again.

**What the lesson must establish:** that these are not trivia. Every one of them is a constraint that
turns into cargo tonnes, and cargo tonnes are freight.

## 10. Module 8 — Vessel classes

**Rests on module 7.** The names are only memorable once the measurements behind them exist.

- **Dry bulk** — Handysize, Handymax and Supramax, Ultramax, Panamax, Kamsarmax (82,000 dwt, 229 m, named for Port Kamsar), Post-Panamax, Capesize, Newcastlemax, VLOC
- **Tankers** — MR, LR1, Aframax, LR2, Suezmax, VLCC, ULCC
- **Gas** — VLGC for LPG at 84,000 to 93,000 cbm; LNG carrier
- **Container** — feeder, Panamax, Neopanamax at around 14,000 TEU, ULCV at 24,000 TEU
- **Panamax** — 294.13 m by 32.31 m, the old Panama locks
- **Neopanamax** — 366 m by 51.25 m, the new Panama locks, 2016
- **TEU** — twenty-foot equivalent unit; a 40 ft box is 2 TEU

**What the lesson must establish:** that the classes are named after their constraints, not their
sizes. Panamax is a lock, Suezmax is a canal cross-section, Kamsarmax is a berth at Port Kamsar,
Newcastlemax is a berth at Newcastle, and Aframax is a freight-rate scale. Learning them as arbitrary
tonnage bands is learning them the hard way.

**Table required** (teaching copy style §4): each class against its deadweight range, its defining
constraint, and its typical trade.

## 11. Module 9 — Canals and chokepoints

**Rests on modules 7 and 8.** Where the dimensions become geography and geography becomes ton-miles.

- **Panama** — a lock canal; Gatun Lake is the summit level so rainfall sets the draft limit. Design maximum 15.24 m TFW for Neopanamax, cut at times to around 14.5 m. Old locks 12.04 m. Air draft 57.91 m
- **Suez** — sea level, no locks. Draft is traded against beam via a submerged cross-section cap of around 1,006 m², so a maximum 20.1 m draft applies only under 50 m beam. Air draft 68 m
- **SUMED** — the pipeline bypass; laden VLCCs part-discharge into it
- **Cape routing** — the Suez alternative; adds ton-miles and tightens effective supply
- **Malacca max** — the draft ceiling through the Straits, around 25 m
- **Bosphorus / Turkish Straits** — the grain chokepoint

**Confidence note.** The Panama draft limit moves with rainfall and the Suez cross-section rules have
been revised more than once. Every figure in this module is marked with the date it was true, per
teaching copy style §5, and the Panama restriction is `varies` by construction.

**What the lesson must establish:** the link back to module 6. A closed or restricted canal does not
reduce the cargo, it lengthens the voyage, which raises ton-mile demand and absorbs tonnage. That is
how a canal becomes a freight rate.

## 12. Module 10 — Ballast and cargo operations

**Rests on module 7.** What actually happens alongside, and why the ship is often waiting.

- **Ballast water** — seawater carried when empty, for stability, propeller immersion and hull stress
- **Ballast leg** — the unpaid empty voyage to the next cargo
- **Triangulation** — chaining cargoes to minimise ballast miles
- **Deballasting** — pumping out while loading; often the bottleneck at fast ore and coal terminals
- **Deballasting rate warranty** — a charterparty or terminal clause requiring full deballast within, for example, 24 hours
- **Gravity discharge** — a bulker's topside tanks drain overboard without pumps
- **Stripping** — clearing the last residual water by eductor
- **BWTS / BWM Convention D-2** — mandatory ballast water treatment; it can throttle flow rates
- **Loading sequence / loading manual** — the plan that keeps shear force and bending moment inside limits

**What the lesson must establish:** the ballast leg is the term that connects this module to the time
charter equivalent in module 6. Ballast days are paid for by nobody and divided into the same voyage
result, which is why triangulation is worth a desk's attention.

---

## 13. Module 11 — Paper, insurance and compliance

**Rests on modules 1 and 3.** What the deal is evidenced by, who covers what when it goes wrong, and
who can stop the ship.

- **B/L, bill of lading** — receipt, evidence of contract, and document of title
- **P&I club** — mutual liability insurance: cargo claims, pollution, crew
- **H&M** — hull and machinery insurance, for damage to the ship itself
- **Class society** — Lloyd's Register, DNV, ABS; certifies structural and mechanical fitness
- **PSC, port state control** — the inspection regime; it can detain
- **Standard CP forms** — GENCON for voyage dry cargo, NYPE 2015 for time charter, BALTIME, SHELLTIME, ASBATANKVOY, BPVOY

**What the lesson must establish:** the three functions of a bill of lading are not a list, they are
three separate legal consequences, and the document of title function is the one that causes trouble.
And that P&I and hull cover answer different questions: damage the ship caused, against damage the
ship suffered.

## 14. Module 12 — Braemar

**Rests on everything.** The point at which the vocabulary becomes a place of work.

- **Shipbroking** — dry cargo, tankers, gas; the chartering desks
- **S&P, sale and purchase** — brokering the ships themselves rather than the cargo
- **Newbuilding** — brokering yard orders
- **Securities / futures** — the forward freight agreement and derivatives desk
- **Risk advisory** — the insurance and risk side
- **One Strand** — the London office

**What the lesson must establish:** which module of this syllabus each desk lives in. Chartering is
modules 2 to 6, securities is the forward freight agreement from module 6, risk advisory is module
11, and sale and purchase is the one desk this syllabus does not otherwise cover, which is the first
item in the proposals below.

**Sourcing note.** Everything in this module describes a real firm and is held to charter MT1 without
exception. Nothing about Braemar's structure, offices or desks is published from memory.

## 15. Proposed additions — not in scope until the principal decides

**These are gaps noticed while organising the supplied list, not additions.** Charter §6.3 makes
syllabus scope the principal's decision, so none of them is being written. Each is one line, worst
gap first.

**Sale and purchase mechanics** — module 12 names an entire Braemar desk the syllabus never teaches: memorandum of agreement, inspection, deposit, delivery.

**Bunkers as a fuel, not just a cost line** — VLSFO, HSFO and MGO, the 0.50% sulphur cap and scrubbers, which drive every voyage cost figure in module 6.

**Incoterms** — FOB, CIF and CFR decide whether the buyer or the seller charters the ship, which is who your charterer actually is.

**Sanctions, AIS and the shadow fleet** — the compliance question a tanker desk faces on every fixture and the syllabus does not mention.

**Carbon regulation** — EEXI, CII and EU ETS now sit in charterparty clauses and change what a ship can be fixed for.

**Cargoes themselves** — dry majors and minors, clean against dirty products; module 7 teaches stowage factor without teaching what is being stowed.

**General average, salvage and Lloyd's Open Form** — what happens after a casualty, and who pays.

**Demolition and the recycling market** — light displacement tonnage as a price, and how scrapping sets the supply side.

**Arbitration and governing law** — London arbitration is where a demurrage dispute from module 4 actually ends.

**Port agency and the ETA sequence** — ETA, ETB and ETD, the vocabulary of the messages a broker reads all day.

## 16. How this is verified

The syllabus is not finished when it is written. Charter §5.2 obligations 1 and 2 bind it directly:

- **Every module here exists in the content tree, and every content module appears here.** Drift in
  either direction fails a content test. This document is the specification, so it is what the test
  compares against.
- **Every taught claim carries a source and a confidence mark.** A content test walks the tree and
  fails on the first item missing either.
- **Every worked example named in this document is computed in a test**, not asserted against its own
  published figure.

**Neither test exists yet**, because the content tree does not (E005). Stating the obligation here is
how the scaffold gets built against it rather than around it.
